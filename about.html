About

I'm a clinical psychologist-in-training focused on trauma, meaning, and the question of how people rebuild after the worst things happen to them.

I took a long road to get here. I started as an enlisted soldier, attended West Point, and led an infantry platoon in Afghanistan. In 2012, an IED ended my time in combat and began a different kind of education — one that started in a hospital and continued through years of asking why some people recover from trauma and others don't. I watched soldiers who witnessed the same event I lived through develop symptoms I never did. That gap stayed with me.

I didn't go straight to psychology. I went to Harvard Business School, worked in finance, healthcare, and technology. But I kept finding my way back to the same conversations — with veterans who weren't just dealing with PTSD, but with something quieter and harder to name: purposelessness, depression, a sense of languishing after leaving service. They talked to me because we shared something. Eventually those conversations became a conviction, and I went back to school.

Now I'm finishing my doctoral training at the VA, working with veterans navigating PTSD, moral injury, and the long aftermath of combat. My research focuses on meaning-in-life as a protective factor against suicide.

How I Think

I don't think any single school of therapy captures the full complexity of a person. My orientation draws from several traditions that, for me, form a coherent whole.

I lean naturally toward cognitive-behavioral work — probably because of a long interest in Stoic philosophy and the idea that how we interpret experience shapes how we suffer. But cognition is only part of the story. People use defenses to keep themselves from feeling things that are painful or threatening, and the real therapeutic work often starts when those defenses come into view. That insight comes from the psychodynamic tradition.

The larger frame is existential. Yalom, Frankl, Becker — they all point to the same thing: people need meaning, and much of what brings someone into therapy traces back to the confrontation with mortality, isolation, or purposelessness. And from ACT, I take the idea that psychological flexibility is a powerful model of health — that identity is shaped by what we repeatedly do, and that lasting change usually begins with behavior.

The thread connecting all of it: a therapist can't change someone's circumstances. What we can change is how they experience those circumstances from the inside — and help them build a life organized around what actually matters to them.
